Your customer story page is the one a buyer opens right before they book the demo, and most of them can't answer the question that buyer is asking: did this actually work, and compared to what? A big percentage with no starting point, no time window, and no date reads like marketing, not proof.

We read 8 SaaS case study examples from Stripe, Vercel, Webflow, Intercom's Fin, Zapier, Calendly, Linear, and AWS, checked each against 12 things a skeptical buyer looks for, then ran the same checks on our own five case studies. Here's what the best pages do, where nearly all of them fall short, and the page structure we'd use instead.

## The short answer: what the best SaaS case study examples do

The strongest SaaS case study examples name the customer in the headline, show two or three results near the top, and quote a named person with a job title. Where most fall short is proof: in our check of 8 pages, only 1 gave a before number, 3 gave a time window, and 1 showed a publish date.

## The 8 customer story pages we tore down

## What the 8 pages get right

The basics are in good shape. If you're building your first customer story page, copy these habits.

- Customer context: 8 of 8 say what the customer does, and most add size or stage (Zapier's sidebar says "1-10" employees, Webflow's says "Mid-market").

- Customer in the headline: 7 of 8. Linear's Middesk story is the exception, with a quote as the headline.

- Product features named: 7 of 8. Stripe ties each result to a named product, so a reader knows what to buy.

- Results near the top: 6 of 8 put a stat block before the story. Webflow's Ramp page shows three metrics right under the intro.

- Named quote with a job title: 6 of 8. Vercel's Klaviyo story quotes two people, each with a title.

- A clear challenge section: 6 of 8.

Our take: in our experience, buyers skim a customer story before deciding to read it. The pages that put the company, the outcome, and a named person in that first screen win the skim.

## Where most SaaS case study examples fall short

Three checks separate a nice story from proof a buyer can defend to their boss. Almost every page we read misses at least two.

### 1. A before number: 1 of 8

Only Fin's Clay story gives a starting point: a resolution rate that began around 20% and now sits between 45% and 50%. Every other page reports a change with nothing to anchor it. "50% fewer missed meetings" could mean 10 down to 5 or 1,000 down to 500, and a buyer can't tell which.

### 2. A time window for the result: 3 of 8

Vercel (two weeks), Stripe (over the past year), and Webflow (page views up 26% in two months) say how long the result took. The other five leave you guessing whether it took a month or three years. Even the Clay page, the only one with a baseline, never says how long the climb from 20% took.

### 3. A visible date: 1 of 8

Only the Vercel page shows when it was published (24 Sep 2026). The rest show a copyright year in the footer and nothing else.

For a buyer, an undated story about a fast-moving product could be four years old. Google also recommends showing a user-visible date labeled "Published" or "Last updated" in its [byline date guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/publication-dates), so a date helps search too.

Put those three together and the count drops to zero: none of the 8 pages gives a before number, a time window, and a date for the same result.

### Two smaller gaps worth fixing

Five of 8 link to related stories, and 5 of 8 end the story with a clear next step. Calendly's 1up.ai page, for example, has its "Sign up for free" button above the story but nothing after it, so a convinced reader hits a dead end.

It felt unfair to grade Stripe and not ourselves. So we ran the same checks on the five case studies on our work page: Juno, Unita, Breakthirty, Beyond Hut, and Cayano.

| Time frame stated | 1 of 5 |

| Before state described | 1 of 5 |

| A client business result as a number | 0 of 5 |

| A visible date | 0 of 5 |

Honestly, we have the same gaps. Our numbers ("9 pages", "8 countries", "4 steps") describe what we built, not what changed for the client. Only the [Juno case study](https://www.natwic.com/work/juno) has a timeline (one week), a client quote, and a described before state.

Part of the reason is real: four of the five were built from scratch, so there's no old site to measure against. That's common for startups, and it's fixable.

For a new build, the baseline is your launch week. Record signups, demo requests, or bookings for the first 30 days, then report the next 60 or 90 against it.

What we'd fix first on our own pages: add a project date to each one, ask each client for a named quote, and add one measured result per client once they're happy to share it.

## The anatomy of a SaaS case study page that earns trust

Here's the order we'd use, built from what the best pages do plus the gaps above. It works for a 400-word story or a 1,500-word one.

![Annotated wireframe of a SaaS case study page showing eight blocks in order, from headline to related stories](/journal/saas-case-study-page-anatomy.webp "saas-case-study-page-anatomy")

1. Headline: customer name plus one result. Using numbers from Fin's own page, "How Clay took its AI resolution rate from 20% to 45-50%" would beat "Transparency and trust".

2. Published date and a one-line company profile (industry, size, region).

3. Result block: two or three numbers, each with a before value and a time window.

4. The challenge: what was broken, in the customer's words, with one number if you have it.

5. Why they chose you: the alternatives they considered. Buyers want to know who you beat.

6. What they did: the specific features or services used, so a reader can map it to their own plan.

7. Results, explained: how each number was measured, plus a named quote with a title.

8. Next step and related stories: one clear call to action, then two stories from similar companies.

## How to write a result line a buyer believes

Most of the fixes above come down to one sentence. Here's the formula we use:

Metric + before + after + time window + how it was measured.

Sample, for an unnamed scheduling tool (not a client):

- Weak: "Cut no-shows by 50%."

- Better: "No-shows fell from 18% to 9% of booked demos in the 90 days after launch, measured in the team's CRM."

The better version is longer, but every part answers a question a skeptical buyer would ask. Use real numbers only, and get the customer's sign-off on every figure before you publish.

There's a legal reason to be careful too. The FTC's [Endorsement Guides FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says that if an advertiser doesn't have proof that an endorser's experience represents what people will generally achieve, the ad must make clear what results people can generally expect. If one customer's result is unusually good, say so.

## FAQ

### How long should a SaaS case study be?

In our sample, length ranged from about 230 words (Linear) to about 1,600 (Fin's Clay story), and both styles work. What matters more is that the first screen carries the customer, the result, and a named person. We'd aim for 500 to 900 words for most stories and go longer only when the buyer is technical.

### What should a SaaS case study include?

At minimum: the customer's name and context, the problem, what they used, two or three results with before numbers and a time window, a named quote with a title, a date, and one next step. The anatomy above puts those in order.

### Should I put results at the top of a case study page?

Yes. Six of the 8 pages we read do it, and it matches how people skim. Put the results in a small block right under the headline, then tell the story underneath for readers who want the detail.

### How do I write a case study when the customer won't share numbers?

Use relative numbers they will approve, like a time saved or a step removed, and say what you measured. A specific operational detail, like Zapier's 46-step automation for Laudable, can also do the work a revenue number would.

### Do case study pages help SEO and AI search?

They can, because they're original content nobody else has. Give each story a descriptive headline, a visible date, and a short summary near the top, so search engines and AI tools can quote the result accurately.

## Make your proof pages work as hard as your product

The best SaaS case study examples we read share one trait: they read like evidence. Your customer stories are some of the few pages on your site a competitor can't copy. Give them a result in the headline, a baseline, a time window, and a date, and they'll do more for your demo pipeline than another feature page.
