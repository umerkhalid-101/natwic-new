You've read the SaaS copywriting tips. Lead with outcomes, add social proof, keep the CTA clear. Then you open a blank doc for your own homepage and still don't know what to write in the hero, how long the page should be, or what to say on the pricing page.

That's not a you problem.

On November 18, 2026, we read seven top-ranking SaaS website copywriting guides side by side and counted what they cover. They agree on the basics and go quiet on the parts founders get stuck on. Below is what they skip, plus how we fill each gap, with a full before/after rewrite of a sample hero.

## The short answer: what SaaS website copywriting needs

Good SaaS website copywriting names one buyer, one painful problem, and one clear outcome, in the buyer's own words. Every top guide we checked says to lead with outcomes. None gives a reading level, covers pricing page copy, or shows a full before/after rewrite. Those gaps are where most startup sites lose people.

## What every SaaS copywriting guide agrees on

The good news: the basics are settled. If you only fix these three things, your site will already read better than most.

### Lead with the outcome

All seven guides say it. Kalungi uses a "Pain, Claim, Gain" framework, Rock The Rankings walks from attention to problem to solution to proof to CTA, and MADX offers headline formulas like "[Core proposition] without the [limitation]."

Our take: write the outcome as something the buyer could repeat to their boss. "Close the books in two days" beats "AI-powered financial operations" every time.

### Steal words from your customers

Six of seven guides tell you to interview customers, read reviews, or listen to sales calls. This is the step founders skip most, because it feels slow. It's also the fastest way to stop writing like a pitch deck.

### Show proof with numbers

Six of seven list specific proof as a tip: a named customer, a quantified result, a real usage number. One vague logo strip doesn't count as much as one customer who says what changed.

## The five gaps, and how we fill them

This is where the guides go quiet, and where we spend most of our time on real projects.

### Gap 1: pricing page copy (0 of 7)

The pricing page is where a buyer decides, and no guide we read covers its copy. Here's what we write on every plan card:

- Who the plan is for, in one line ("For teams of 2 to 10")

- The one limit that pushes people to upgrade, stated plainly

- A button label that says what happens next ("Start free" or "Talk to us")

Then add a short FAQ right on the page that answers billing questions: what happens when the trial ends, whether you can cancel anytime, and how seats are counted. Those are objections, which brings us to gap 2.

### Gap 2: objections (2 of 7)

Only Position Digital and Linkflow tell you to answer objections in the copy. On a SaaS site, the same five come up again and again:

1. "Switching will take forever." Say how long setup takes and what you import.

2. "Is our data safe?" Name your security basics in plain words and link to the details.

3. "My team won't use it." Show the first screen they'll see, not a feature grid.

4. "It's too expensive." Compare it to the cost of the problem, not to competitors.

5. "What if it doesn't work for us?" State your trial, refund, or cancel terms.

Answer each one where it naturally comes up, not in a wall at the bottom.

### Gap 3: a reading level (0 of 7)

Two guides mention Hemingway or Grammarly, but none gives a target. Our take: aim for about grade 8 or lower on your homepage, even for technical buyers. Smart people read plain copy faster too.

The old Process Street study of [87 SaaS startup landing pages](https://www.process.st/startup-landing-pages/) (published in 2016) found 42% of headlines contained jargon terms. It also counted 65 uses of "you" or "your" against just 6 of "we" or "our," so even then, the better pages talked to the reader.

### Gap 4: how long the page should be (0 of 7)

No guide gives a number, and for good reason: there isn't a right one. Copyhackers looked for tests and found that [no case studies cleanly test short copy against long](https://copyhackers.com/2020/09/saas-websites-is-shorter-copy-really-better/). In one Crazy Egg test they cite, a page about 20 times longer won by 64%, and a later, much shorter version won after that.

Our take: length follows awareness. If buyers already know the category, keep it short. If you're explaining a new idea, you need more words, broken into scannable sections. In a 1997 study, Nielsen Norman Group found that [79% of test users scanned new pages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) and only 16% read word by word, so headings carry the load.

### Gap 5: in-product microcopy (0 of 7)

Your website copy doesn't stop at the signup button. The signup form, the first empty state, and the first error message are all copy, and none of the seven guides covers them.

Three quick rules we use: ask only for what you need on signup, make every empty state tell people the one next step, and write errors that say how to fix the problem, not just that one exists.

## A before/after SaaS website copywriting rewrite

None of the guides shows a full rewrite, so here's one. This is a sample hero for an invoicing tool, written the way we see most startup heroes written, then rewritten with everything above.

![Before and after rewrite of a sample SaaS hero: vague AI platform headline vs a specific outcome, buyer, and free signup](/journal/saas-hero-copy-before-after-rewrite.webp "saas-hero-copy-before-after-rewrite")

Before:

- Headline: The AI-powered platform that streamlines your financial operations.

- Subheadline: Unify invoicing, payments, and reporting in one all-in-one solution built for modern teams.

- Button: Request a demo

After:

- Headline: Send an invoice in 60 seconds. Let reminders chase the late ones.

- Subheadline: For agencies and freelancers billing 10 to 200 clients a month. Connect Stripe or your bank and get paid without the follow-up emails.

- Button: Start free, no card needed

- Proof line: one real number from your product, like invoices sent last month

What changed: the headline names an outcome a buyer can picture, the subheadline names who it's for, the button removes the price objection, and "AI-powered" is gone, because the buyer cares what it does, not how. Only use a number like "60 seconds" if your product really does it.

## Writing copy that AI search can quote

Only Position Digital and Linkflow cover AI search. As more founders ask ChatGPT or Google's AI Overviews to compare tools, your copy needs lines a model can lift as is.

- Answer the main question in the first two sentences of each section.

- Use specific nouns: who it's for, what it replaces, what it integrates with.

- Put real numbers next to the claims they support.

- Keep a plain FAQ on key pages, written in the questions buyers actually ask.

These habits help human readers too, which is the point.

## FAQ

### What is SaaS website copywriting?

It's the writing on a SaaS company's marketing site: the homepage, product pages, pricing page, and signup flow. Its job is to make one type of buyer understand the problem you solve and take the next step, usually a free signup or a demo.

### How do you write a SaaS homepage headline?

Name the outcome your buyer wants in plain words, then use the subheadline to say who it's for and how it works. Pull the wording from customer interviews where you can. Position Digital suggests 6 to 12 words for a headline, and the Process Street study found an average of 6.

### How long should SaaS website copy be?

There's no proven ideal length. Copyhackers found no clean tests of short against long copy. Match the length to how well buyers already understand your category, and break long pages into short, scannable sections.

### Should a startup hire a SaaS copywriter or write the copy itself?

Founders know the buyer best, so do the customer research and a first draft yourself. A copywriter or studio is worth it when the draft is clear to you but not to strangers, or when the site is about to get paid traffic. Either way, the research step can't be skipped.

### What's the most common SaaS copywriting mistake?

Describing the technology instead of the result. In our experience, phrases like "AI-powered platform" or "all-in-one solution" show up in early drafts because they're easy to write, and buyers skim right past them.
