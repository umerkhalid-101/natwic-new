You know your startup should be on social media. You also know the last three months of your company page look like a launch announcement, a hiring post, and a long silence.

The fix isn't posting more. It's a social media strategy for startups that fits on one page: one goal, two channels, a few topics, and a pace your team can actually keep. Below you'll find the template, a filled example, what the 2026 engagement data says about which formats to post, and a 90-day rollout.

## The short answer: social media strategy for startups

A social media strategy for startups is a one-page plan covering six things: one business goal, one audience, two channels, three or four content pillars, a weekly cadence you can sustain for 90 days, and three metrics you review monthly. Start small, post consistently, and expand only what works.

## Why most startup social media plans stall

Most early social accounts don't fail because of bad content. They stall because the plan asks for more than a small team can deliver.

We compared the posting advice in guides that rank for this topic, and the range is wild:

The first plan adds up to at least 35 posts a week. The last one starts at a single post.

Our take: a founder with a product to ship won't hit 35 posts a week, and a plan you drop in week three does less than a smaller plan you keep all year. In our experience, most accounts need a few months of steady posting before engagement builds, so the plan has to survive well past month one.

The other common stall points:

- Trying to be on five platforms at once, with nothing tailored to any of them.

- Posting without a goal, so nobody can tell whether it's working.

- Designing every post from scratch, which turns a 20-minute task into a two-hour one.

Fill in each box with one sentence or a short list. If a box needs a paragraph, the plan is getting too big.

### 1. One goal tied to the business

Pick the single outcome social should move this quarter: demo requests, waitlist signups, hiring, or investor credibility. "Grow our following" isn't a goal. It's a side effect.

Write it as a number with a deadline, like "20 demo requests from LinkedIn by March 31."

### 2. One audience, described in a sentence

"Heads of operations at 50 to 500 person logistics companies" beats "B2B decision makers." The tighter the description, the easier every post gets, because you know exactly who you're writing to.

### 3. Two channels, not five

Choose where your buyers already spend time. For most B2B and SaaS startups, that's LinkedIn plus one more. For consumer products and local businesses, it's usually Instagram plus one more.

Buffer's State of Social Media Engagement 2026 report, which analyzed more than 52 million posts, found LinkedIn had the highest median engagement rate in its dataset, at about 6.1%. Claim your handle everywhere, but only post on the two channels you picked.

### 4. Three or four content pillars

Pillars are the recurring topics you post about. A simple mix for a startup:

- Problem: the pain your customer feels, in their words.

- Proof: product updates, customer wins, and behind-the-scenes builds.

- Point of view: what your team believes about your industry.

- People: the founders, the team, and hiring.

Rotate them so the feed never turns into one long sales pitch.

### 5. A cadence you can keep for 90 days

Plan for your busiest week, not your quietest. For most early teams, that's two or three posts a week on the main channel and one to three on the second.

Even one weekly post on your company page is a real start, and it's the pace LinkedIn's own [Page best practices](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/linkedin-pages/linkedin-company-page-best-practices) tie to stronger engagement.

### 6. Three metrics you review monthly

Track one metric for reach (impressions or followers), one for engagement (comments and shares, not just likes), and one tied to your goal (site visits, demo requests, or signups).

Add a reply rule too. About 75% of consumers expect a reply within 24 hours or less, according to the 2025 Sprout Social Index cited in [Sprout Social's customer service statistics](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/social-media-customer-service-statistics/).

## Which social media post formats work best in 2026

Buffer's [2026 analysis of content formats](https://buffer.com/resources/data-best-content-format-social-media/) shows a big gap between formats on the two channels most startups choose.

![ Bar chart of 2026 median engagement by format: LinkedIn carousels 21.77% vs text 3.18%, Instagram carousels 6.9%](/journal/social-media-engagement-by-format-2026.webp "social-media-engagement-by-format-2026")

Here's what we take from it:

- On LinkedIn, carousels earned nearly 7 times the median engagement rate of text-only posts.

- Link posts sit near the bottom, so share the idea in the post itself and put the link in the first comment.

- On Instagram, Reels had the lowest engagement rate but the biggest reach. Buffer found they reached 2.25 times as many people as single images. Use Reels to get discovered and carousels to teach.

That's good news for a small team. A carousel is a design job, not a film shoot, and in our experience one solid template turns a blog post or customer story into five slides in about an hour.

## Founder profile or company page?

Both, because they do different jobs. The company page is your proof shelf: product news, customer stories, carousels, and hiring. It's where a buyer or investor checks that you're real and active.

The founder profile is the voice: opinions, lessons, and the story behind the product. In our experience, founder posts start more conversations, because people follow people.

Keep the two visually consistent. The same colors, type, and templates on both make every post recognizable before anyone reads the name. ## Your first 90 days

- Days 1 to 30, set up: fill in the one-page plan, update both profiles (photo, banner, bio, and a link to your site), build three to five post templates, and start your cadence.

- Days 31 to 60, learn: check which pillar and format earned the most comments, then do more of that. Drop one thing that isn't working.

- Days 61 to 90, commit: batch a full month of posts in one sitting, and decide whether the second channel has earned its place.

## FAQ

### How often should a startup post on social media?

Start with a pace you can keep for 90 days. Two or three posts a week on your main channel and one to three on your second is a realistic range for a small team. LinkedIn reports that companies posting weekly see a 2x lift in engagement, so consistency matters more than volume.

### Which social media platform is best for B2B startups?

For most B2B and SaaS startups, LinkedIn. It had the highest median engagement rate in Buffer's 2026 report, at about 6.1%, and it's where buyers, hires, and investors check you out. Add one more channel, like X or Instagram, based on where your audience spends time.

### How long does it take to see results from social media?

Most accounts need a few months of consistent posting before engagement builds. Judge the plan at the 90-day mark, not after two weeks, and track a goal metric like demo requests alongside followers.

### Should a startup run paid social ads from day one?

Usually not. Organic posting shows you which messages and formats people respond to, and ads can then amplify the posts that already work. If you need leads fast, run a small paid test once you have a landing page that converts.

### What is the 5-5-5 rule for social media?

It's a weekly routine some guides recommend, and definitions vary slightly: create five posts, engage with five posts from other accounts, and connect with five new people. It's a good engagement habit, but five posts a week is a lot for a team of two, so adjust the first number to your cadence.
